Amoxicillin, sold under the brand name Amoxil, is a semi-synthetic broad-spectrum antibiotic of the penicillin family. It is among the most widely prescribed antibacterials in the world thanks to reliable oral absorption, a well-established safety record and activity against many common respiratory, urinary, dental and skin pathogens.

How it works

Amoxicillin is bactericidal — it kills bacteria rather than merely stopping them multiplying. It binds penicillin-binding proteins in the bacterial cell wall and blocks the final cross-linking step of peptidoglycan synthesis. Without an intact wall the bacterium cannot resist its own internal pressure and bursts.

Amoxicillin is not stable against beta-lactamase, an enzyme some resistant bacteria produce. That is why it is often paired with clavulanic acid (co-amoxiclav) when such organisms are suspected.

What it treats

  • Ear, nose and throat — acute otitis media, bacterial sinusitis, tonsillitis, strep throat
  • Chest and airways — community-acquired pneumonia, acute exacerbations of chronic bronchitis
  • Urinary tract — infections caused by susceptible E. coli, Proteus mirabilis or Enterococcus faecalis
  • Skin and dental — cellulitis, dental and odontogenic abscess
  • H. pylori eradication — with a proton-pump inhibitor plus clarithromycin or metronidazole
  • Early Lyme disease — an alternative to doxycycline, especially in children and pregnancy
  • Endocarditis prophylaxis — a single dose before certain dental work in high-risk cardiac patients

Key facts

Drug classAminopenicillin (beta-lactam antibiotic)
Common strengths250 mg, 500 mg, 875 mg tablets and capsules; 125 mg/5 mL and 250 mg/5 mL suspension
Time to peak level1–2 hours
Half-lifeAbout 1–1.5 hours
AvailabilityPrescription only
Antibiotics do not treat viral illness Amoxicillin has no effect on colds, flu, COVID-19 or most sore throats and coughs, which are viral. Taking it unnecessarily drives antibiotic resistance and exposes you to side effects for no benefit.
Do not take if you are allergic to penicillins Serious and occasionally fatal anaphylactic reactions have occurred. Tell your doctor about any previous reaction to penicillin, amoxicillin, ampicillin or a cephalosporin before you start.

Tell your doctor before use if you have

  • Any drug allergy, particularly to penicillins or cephalosporins, or a history of severe skin reactions
  • Infectious mononucleosis (mono) — amoxicillin causes a widespread measles-like rash in a high proportion of these patients and should be avoided
  • Kidney disease — the dose and interval need adjusting; the 875 mg tablet is unsuitable if creatinine clearance is under 30 mL/min
  • Liver disease or previous jaundice with an antibiotic
  • Asthma, hay fever or eczema — an atopic history slightly raises allergy risk
  • Phenylketonuria (PKU) — some chewable and dispersible forms contain aspartame
  • Colitis or previous antibiotic-associated diarrhea

While you are taking it

  • Stop and get emergency care for hives, swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, wheezing, trouble breathing, or a blistering or peeling rash.
  • Persistent, watery or bloody diarrhea — during treatment or up to two months afterward — may signal C. difficile colitis. Do not take anti-diarrheal medicine; call your doctor.
  • Prolonged or repeated courses can allow overgrowth of resistant organisms or yeast, causing oral thrush or vaginal candidiasis.
  • Drink plenty of fluids to reduce the small risk of amoxicillin crystals forming in the urine.
  • It can cause a false-positive urine glucose test with copper-reduction methods (Benedict's or Fehling's). Enzyme-based strips are unaffected.

Medicines that interact

  • Allopurinol — sharply increases the chance of a skin rash
  • Methotrexate — amoxicillin slows its clearance and can cause serious toxicity
  • Warfarin — INR may rise, increasing bleeding risk; check it more often
  • Probenecid — raises and prolongs amoxicillin blood levels
  • Live oral typhoid vaccine — may be inactivated; separate by at least three days
  • Combined oral contraceptives — a small drop in effectiveness is possible; consider backup contraception during the course and for seven days after

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and alcohol

Amoxicillin is among the antibiotics most commonly used in pregnancy and animal studies have not shown harm, but use it only on medical advice. Small amounts pass into breast milk and may cause loose stools or thrush in the infant. Amoxicillin has no direct interaction with alcohol — unlike metronidazole — though alcohol can worsen nausea and dehydration while you are unwell.

Driving

Amoxicillin does not usually affect driving, but dizziness has been reported. See how it affects you first.

Most people tolerate amoxicillin well. Effects are grouped by how often they occur — appearing on this list does not mean you will experience them.

Common Usually mild

  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Diarrhea or loose stools
  • Stomach discomfort or bloating
  • Headache
  • Altered or metallic taste
  • Mild skin rash

Less common Tell your doctor

  • Oral thrush or vaginal yeast infection
  • Itching or hives
  • Black "hairy"-looking tongue and surface tooth staining, usually reversible with brushing
  • Dizziness, trouble sleeping or restlessness
  • Temporary rise in liver enzymes

Serious — get help immediately Emergency

  • Anaphylaxis or angioedema — swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, chest tightness, trouble breathing, collapse
  • Severe skin reactions — Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, AGEP, or DRESS (rash with fever and swollen glands)
  • C. difficile colitis — severe, persistent or bloody diarrhea with cramps and fever
  • Liver injury — yellowing of skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools
  • Kidney inflammation — reduced urine output, blood in urine, flank pain
  • Blood disorders — unusual bruising or bleeding, sore throat, extreme fatigue
  • Seizures — mainly at high doses in people with impaired kidney function

This is not a complete list. Report anything unexpected or persistent to your doctor or pharmacist. In the United States you can also report side effects to the FDA at 1-800-FDA-1088.

Finish the whole course Take every dose even once you feel better. Stopping early lets the toughest bacteria survive and multiply, causing relapse and encouraging resistant strains.

Taking it correctly

  • Swallow capsules and tablets whole with a full glass of water. Do not crush or chew extended-release tablets.
  • May be taken with or without food. Taking it with a meal reduces nausea.
  • Space doses evenly — every 8 or 12 hours as prescribed — to hold blood levels steady.
  • Shake the oral suspension well before each dose and measure with the supplied syringe or cup, never a kitchen spoon.
  • Chewable tablets must be chewed or crushed completely before swallowing.

Children and special situations

  • Children are dosed by weight, typically 25–45 mg/kg/day split every 8–12 hours, up to 80–90 mg/kg/day for ear infections.
  • Kidney impairment requires a lower dose and longer interval below 30 mL/min creatinine clearance.
  • Hemodialysis removes amoxicillin, so an extra dose is given during and after the session.

Missed dose

Take it as soon as you remember. If the next dose is nearly due, skip the missed one and carry on as normal — never double up.

Overdose

Usually causes nausea, vomiting and diarrhea; at very high doses, crystals in the urine and kidney injury. Contact Poison Control (1-800-222-1222 in the US) or emergency services. Treatment is supportive and amoxicillin can be cleared by dialysis.

Storage

  • Store tablets and capsules at 20–25 °C (68–77 °F), in the original container, away from heat, light and moisture. A bathroom cabinet is not suitable.
  • Reconstituted suspension goes in the refrigerator and is discarded after 14 days, even if some is left.
  • Keep out of reach of children. Do not use past the expiry date, and never take leftover antibiotic from a previous illness.
  • Return unused medicine to a pharmacy take-back program rather than flushing it or binning it.
How long does amoxicillin take to work?

Most people notice improvement within 48 to 72 hours. If you are no better after three full days, or you get worse at any point, contact your doctor — the bacteria may be resistant or the infection may not be bacterial at all.

Can I stop once I feel better?

No. Feeling better means the most susceptible bacteria have died; the hardiest ones are still there. Stopping early lets them repopulate and raises the chance of a relapse that is harder to treat. Finish exactly what you were prescribed.

Can I drink alcohol on amoxicillin?

Amoxicillin has no direct interaction with alcohol, so an occasional drink will not cause a reaction the way it does with metronidazole. That said, alcohol worsens dehydration and nausea and disrupts sleep, none of which helps you recover.

Does it affect birth control pills?

The evidence for a meaningful interaction is weak, and rifampin is the only antibiotic clearly shown to reduce oral contraceptive effectiveness. Many clinicians still advise a backup method during the course and for seven days after, particularly if vomiting or diarrhea occur — those definitely reduce absorption.

I got a rash a few days in. Is it an allergy?

Not necessarily. A flat, blotchy rash appearing after several days is often a non-allergic reaction, especially in people who have mono. A true allergy usually appears sooner and involves raised, itchy hives, swelling or breathing difficulty. Either way, stop and speak to your doctor — do not decide this on your own.

What is the difference between amoxicillin and Augmentin?

Augmentin is amoxicillin plus clavulanic acid, which blocks the beta-lactamase enzyme some bacteria use to destroy amoxicillin. It covers more organisms but causes noticeably more diarrhea. Doctors reserve it for infections where resistant bacteria are likely.

Can I take it while pregnant or breastfeeding?

Amoxicillin is one of the antibiotics most often used in pregnancy and is generally considered acceptable when needed. Small amounts pass into breast milk and may give the baby loose stools or thrush. Always confirm with your own doctor.
